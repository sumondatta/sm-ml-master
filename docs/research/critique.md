# Completeness critique of the research sweep

<!-- Produced by a critic agent asked what the sweep MISSED. -->

## Assessment

The sweep is strong on inventory (gridded covariates, soil and salinity products, irrigation-extent maps, literature-mining APIs, ML architectures, warehouse engineering) and weak on everything that is adjudication, provenance-in-law, label truth, and operability. Specifically: (a) the "networks" researcher returned nothing usable, so the single largest in-situ source category is covered only incidentally through the "papers" list; (b) there is zero legal analysis, which is fatal because the deliverable is a redistributable database built by mass-harvesting copyrighted PDFs and re-plotting their figures; (c) the project's defining variable — is this station-year irrigated, by what method, when, and how much — has maps but no adjudication design, no label-error budget, and no amount/timing reconstruction plan, even though irrigation is the forcing that makes the problem non-trivial; (d) the literature sweep is overwhelmingly English-language, OA, and Anglophone-indexed, while the world's irrigated-agronomy soil water profile literature is disproportionately Chinese, Persian, Turkish, Spanish/Portuguese, Arabic and Indian, and disproportionately in theses, extension bulletins and FAO/IAEA/CGIAR grey literature that OpenAlex, Europe PMC and CORE index poorly; (e) figure digitization has a rich tool list but no accuracy benchmark, no accept/reject threshold and no yield/effort model, so nobody knows whether this produces 300 series or 30,000 and at what error; (f) the physical covariates that actually control multi-depth moisture in irrigated fields — sensor position relative to emitter/bed/furrow, plastic mulch, crop and rooting depth, shallow water tables and capillary rise — are almost entirely absent, while the plan over-invests in yet more 1 km reanalysis; (g) there is no operational specification (what inputs exist at inference time, what accuracy beats a sensor in clay and saline soil, what decision the prediction serves) and no staged plan a single researcher can execute, so the current plan is a maximalist wish list with a high probability of becoming a multi-year data-engineering sinkhole that never reaches steps 2 and 3. The six gaps are ranked by how likely each is to invalidate or stall the project. Two further items are worth noting but did not make the cut as standalone gaps: cross-sensor harmonisation is well covered by the "sensors" researcher in principle but has no empirical validation plan of its own (it should be folded into the digitization-benchmark work), and leakage-free evaluation is well specified in the "ml" digest but is undermined by the unresolved question of what counts as an independent site when the same physical field appears in a network archive, a paper figure and a repository deposit — which the deduplication design in "engineering" addresses at the record level but not at the evaluation-grouping level.

## Gaps


### [severity 1] No legal, licensing or redistribution framework for mass literature harvesting, figure digitization, or reuse of network data — the step-one deliverable may be unpublishable as built.

**Why it matters.** The plan bulk-downloads hundreds of thousands of publisher PDFs, extracts numbers from copyrighted figures, and publishes the result openly. Three concrete failure modes: (1) the university's Elsevier/Wiley/Springer licences get suspended when the library IP range is caught bulk-downloading — the most common way TDM projects die; (2) the database cannot be deposited because it mixes CC-BY-NC, ODbL share-alike, research-use-only and DUA-restricted rows with no per-row licence tracking, contaminating the whole table; (3) in-situ network terms (ISMN, AmeriFlux, CERN, TPDC, national met services) often require per-network citation, provider notification, or forbid redistribution outright, and violating those damages the author's standing in the community he built his career in. None of the eight researchers touched any of this.


### [severity 1] No design for the project's defining label: whether a station-year is irrigated, by what method, on what dates, with how much water — and no error budget for getting it wrong.

**Why it matters.** The user said irrigated fields 'supersede everything', yet the sweep returned ~20 irrigation-extent rasters and no adjudication logic. Extent maps answer a different question: a 30 m LANID pixel says a pixel was irrigated in a year; the project needs to know whether the soil volume around a sensor received water on a given day. LANID/IrrMapper/LGRIP30 carry commission/omission errors of roughly 5-20% concentrated exactly where it matters (small fields, supplemental irrigation, mixed pixels, non-CONUS); a station can sit in an irrigated field that was fallow that season; a pivot-corner sensor may never be wetted; drip and furrow maps say nothing about whether the sensor is in the wetted bulb. And if irrigation dates and amounts are unknown for most literature-derived series — they will be — the model is asked to predict the consequence of an unobserved forcing and will learn climatology plus a site offset. These rows do not add noise, they bias the model against exactly the wet conditions the project exists to serve.


### [severity 1] The literature sweep is Anglophone, OA-biased and journal-only — it structurally misses the Chinese, Persian, Turkish, Spanish/Portuguese, Arabic and Indian irrigation literature and the thesis/extension/FAO-IAEA-CGIAR grey literature, where most of the world's irrigated soil water profiles actually live.

**Why it matters.** The user asked to sift the literature of the whole world. The litmine stack (OpenAlex, Crossref, Semantic Scholar, Europe PMC, CORE, Unpaywall, arXiv, DOAJ, BASE) is an excellent Western-journal stack that is close to blind to the highest-density sources for this topic. Irrigated agronomy with multi-depth soil water profiles is concentrated in China, Iran, India, Egypt, Turkey, Pakistan, Spain and Brazil, much of it indexed only in CNKI/Wanfang/CSCD, SID.ir/Magiran, DergiPark/TR Dizin, SciELO/Redalyc, J-STAGE and KCI. Beyond language, the densest vein is grey literature: MSc/PhD theses (nearly every irrigation thesis contains multi-depth neutron-probe or TDR profiles, often in appendix TABLES, which are far more accurate to extract than figures), state experiment-station and extension reports (the digest already stumbled into Kansas State's New Prairie Press as a one-off), FAO/IAEA TECDOCs, CGIAR centre outputs, and national institute repositories (Embrapa, ICAR KRISHI, CAAS). This is not a marginal recall loss — it probably means missing the majority of the target population while claiming global scope.


### [severity 1] Figure digitization has a tool list but no accuracy benchmark, no accept/reject criterion, and no yield or effort model — the project's novel contribution is unvalidated and unsized.

**Why it matters.** Digitization is the differentiator and the biggest technical risk, yet it is treated as solved tooling: PyMuPDF vector extraction, Grobid, LineFormer, DePlot, ChartGemma, plus 'uncertainty quantification (recalled)'. Missing is all of the empirical part. Nobody knows what fraction of target PDFs have vector rather than raster figures — one number, knowable today by sampling 200 papers, that moves the error floor by an order of magnitude. Nobody has measured method error against ground truth using papers that published both figure and data. Nobody has addressed this domain's specific chart pathologies: 4-6 overlapping depth series, error bars, precipitation bars hanging inverted from a top axis, broken and log axes, and the 2D moisture-salinity contour plots several of the listed Iranian and Chinese papers use, which none of the listed tools handle. And nobody has produced a yield estimate, so it is unknown whether this returns 300 series or 30,000 — or whether simply emailing 200 corresponding authors would beat it per hour spent. The reviewer's first question, 'how do you know the digitized values are right?', currently has no answer.


### [severity 1] The covariates that physically control multi-depth moisture in irrigated fields are largely absent: sensor position relative to the wetting geometry (emitter/bed/furrow/pivot), plastic mulch, crop and rooting depth over time, tillage/compaction, and shallow water table with capillary rise.

**Why it matters.** The sweep invested heavily in 1 km reanalysis and global soil rasters and almost nothing in the field-scale physics that dominates the variance it must predict. Under drip, two sensors 30 cm apart at the same depth on the same day routinely differ by 0.10-0.20 m3/m3 depending on distance from the emitter — larger than the entire model error budget. Under furrow, bed-top versus furrow-bottom placement is two different regimes. Film-mulched drip cotton in Xinjiang, already a large share of the papers list, has near-zero soil evaporation under the film, so any model with a bare-soil evaporation prior is wrong there. Rooting depth and LAI evolve through the season and set where water is extracted, which is exactly what a multi-depth model must get right, and static texture cannot encode it. In the world's major irrigation districts with shallow water tables — Nile Delta, Indus, Fergana, San Joaquin, parts of Xinjiang and the North China Plain — capillary rise supplies much of the deep-layer water and co-drives the salinity the user cares about, yet no water-table dataset appears anywhere in the digest. The model will therefore be unable to distinguish 'wet at 60 cm because irrigated' from 'wet at 60 cm because the water table is at 120 cm', which undercuts the sensor-replacement claim.


### [severity 1] No operational specification and no staged execution plan: nobody defined what inputs exist at prediction time, what accuracy would actually beat a sensor in the clay and saline soils that motivate the project, or what a single researcher builds first.

**Why it matters.** The stated goal is to replace or minimise sensors precisely because sensors fail in high-clay and saline soils — a falsifiable claim with a numeric threshold that appears nowhere in the sweep. The ML researcher gave architectures, CV protocols and metrics but no operational contract: is the model run with no in-situ input (true replacement), seeded with occasional gravimetric samples (reduction), or forecasting forward from yesterday's reading (scheduling aid)? Those are three different models with three different feasible accuracies, and the digest's own leakage warning makes the distinction decisive. Also absent: decision-level evaluation — scheduling triggers on crossing a depletion threshold, so what matters is whether the trigger day is right, not pooled RMSE; and a head-to-head error budget showing model error in a saline clay is smaller than sensor error there, which is the entire premise. Finally, the combined sweep describes roughly a 15-person-year programme handed to one researcher. There is no minimum viable slice, no milestone sequence, no compute budget and no stopping rule. The likeliest real failure is not a wrong model — it is never shipping one because step one becomes a two-year data-engineering sinkhole.
